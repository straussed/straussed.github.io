Stability and change in animal monarchies

[PLACEHOLDER: theme image — 16:9]

In spotted hyenas and in many cercopithecine primates, dominance is passed from mother to offpsring through social—rather than genetic—inheritance. Offspring acquire positions immediately below their mothers and above their older siblings, creating matrilines that often persist as dynasties across generations. Rank acquired this way shapes access to food, reproductive success, and consequently, fitness. It remains unclear why animals would adhere to apparently arbitrary rules of social inheritance when they stand to benefit from fighting for preferred positions in their society.

Hierarchy dynamics offer a lens into the processes underlying these social structures. Social and demographic processes both drive substantive change in these systems&emdash;although social alliances predict coups, most change an individual experiences is driven by demographic turnover, causing indivdiuals to decline in rank across their lifespans.

Questions we're asking

How do alliances form and dissolve?

What conditions favor the evolution of socially inherited hierarchies?

How does demographic change shape alliance structure and hierarchy stability?

What costs do individuals face during coups?

How is being queen different in kind from being royalty?

Do behavioral differences among queens affect the behavior of the group?

What determines the success or failure of family dynasties?
