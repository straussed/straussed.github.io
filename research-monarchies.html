Stability and change in animal monarchies

In spotted hyenas and in many cercopithecine primates, dominance is passed from mother to offspring through social—rather than genetic—inheritance. Offspring acquire positions immediately below their mothers and above their older siblings, creating matrilines that often persist as dynasties across generations. It is perplexing that animals follow such monarchy-like rules of inheritance rather than competing for more advantageous positions in the social hierarchy, as high rank confers better access to food, reproductive success, and consequently, fitness.

Hierarchy dynamics offer a lens into the processes underlying these social structures. Social and demographic processes both drive substantive change in these systems—although social alliances predict coups, most change an individual experiences is driven by demographic turnover, causing individuals to decline in rank across their lifespans. In these societies, individual fitness is thus shaped by the dynamic dominance trajectories that arise from social and demographic processes operating at the group scale.

Questions we're asking

How do alliances form and dissolve?

What conditions favor the evolution of socially inherited hierarchies?

How does demographic change shape alliance structure and hierarchy stability?

How do ecological conditions shape hierarchy stability and change?

What costs do individuals face during coups?

In what ways are the benefits of being the alpha female different from being merely high ranking?

Do behavioral differences among queens affect the behavior of the group?

What determines the success or failure of family dynasties?
