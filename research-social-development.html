Social development and social competence

[PLACEHOLDER: theme image — 16:9]

For animals that interact with the same group mates for a lifetime, social skill is a critical component of development. Young animals have to learn who is who, what each relationship is worth, when to defer and when to press, and how to recruit help—and they differ enormously in how well they end up doing it. What drives that variation in social competence is largely unresolved.

Societies with social inheritance are unusually good places to ask. Because rank is handed down rather than earned, an individual's social position is set largely independently of its own qualities, which lets us pull apart the effects of position and ability instead of watching them move together. We use that leverage to interrogate the roles of maternal, offspring, and peer-group traits in shaping social development, social competence later in life, and ultimately fitness.

Questions we're asking

What is social competence, and how would we know it when we see it?

What do mothers contribute?

What do offspring bring to it?

How much does the peer group matter?

Does early social experience predict competence in adulthood?

What does social competence buy?
